The Afterlife of a Film: Does the Box Office Have the Last Word?

The ticket counter no longer tells the whole story of a film.

By Sejall Patil, Advita Joshi, Soham Kulkarni, Astha Sawant and Apurva Tupe

We followed Dhurandhar, Raja Shivaji and Dune: Part Two from the theatres to OTT, surveyed 136 viewers and spoke to two film industry professionals. For a long time, a film’s success was linked to its box office performance. The entry of streaming platforms altered this routine and COVID played the catalyst in this shift. The box office still matters. For producers, it remains “the litmus test to see if a film is successful or not”, as filmmaker Nipun Dharmadhikari told us. But viewers are less convinced, and a theatre figure shows only how a film opened.

What the Theatre Does Not Tell Us

Dhurandhar makes the problem immediately visible. It recorded a reported worldwide gross of ₹1,240 crore against a production budget of ₹280 crore, and in the United States its second week earned more than the first. Riddhi Mahashabde, Senior Creative Director at Jio Studios, which produced the film, recalls, “The first three days were nothing. On Friday, Saturday and Monday, there was no business. On Tuesday, Akshaye Khanna’s reel went viral. And after that, the film did history”. Theatre business, she says, rests on “resonance and virality and word of mouth”, which an opening number does not measure.

Raja Shivaji conveys the story of language. The Marathi version accounts for nearly three quarters of its India net collection, even though the Hindi version was released on far more screens. On opening day, the Hindi dub was scheduled in more than twice as many shows as the original, yet it earned well under a third of the day’s money. A Marathi screening, in effect, earned about five times what a Hindi screening did. Ms Mahashabde says that Raja Shivaji “already has sentiments attached to it.”

Dune: Part Two tells the opposite story. It earned more than half of its India net collection in week one, then roughly halved each week. Its Indian theatrical journey saw a burst of interest that arrived early and faded fast. Theatrical numbers also come with a measure that often misleads: occupancy. A café with two tables is “100 percent full” when both are taken. Something similar happened to Dune: Part Two. Occupancy rises as the number of shows falls, so a full hall can be a sign of a film’s decline as easily as of its popularity.

Theatre viewing depends on the calendar too. For both Dune: Part Two and Raja Shivaji, about two thirds of the first week’s earnings came from the first weekend. “Everyone is waiting for Saturdays and Sundays. How are the reviews? What is happening? There is a lot of choosiness,” says Ms Mahashabde.

A New Measure of Success

Theatre counts money, whilst OTT platforms measure attention: hours watched, views and weekly rank. A streaming chart determines whether a film makes a comeback. Streaming platforms look to the theatre: “we will see how you perform in people’s courts, and we will negotiate accordingly,” says Mr Dharmadhikari. Dhurandhar and Raja Shivaji reached platforms seven to eight weeks after release, Dune: Part Two took over five months. About seven in ten of our 136 respondents would wait no longer than two months, a shift Ms Mahashabde attributes to the pandemic.

The Second Life

On Netflix, Dhurandhar opened at the top of the weekly ranking, fell almost 90 percent in viewing between weeks two and seven, then tripled as its sequel reached cinemas. An audience can be reeled back by events unrelated to a film’s release, which a theatrical run cannot do.

Raja Shivaji entered at tenth place, in week two its viewing hours doubled and it rose to third, while its theatre earnings halved each week. Dune: Part Two’s OTT journey stretched furthest: seven languages on Jio Cinema, second place on Max in the US, third in Sky’s UK top five. At home, says Ms Mahashabde, the risk is smaller. A ticket is a bet placed in advance with money, travel and time. “OTT is free for them. If you don’t like the film, you can stop it.”

Why People Go Out and Why They Stay In

Cinema has become an occasion, tied to the shared experience of watching a film on a large screen while OTT fits more easily into everyday routines, with language, time and price chosen at home. In our survey, the trailer ranked first for a theatre visit, followed by reviews and star cast. For OTT films, reviews and ratings came first and theatre success ranked lower.

What earns the big screen is scale, “Anything immersive is working”, says Ms Mahashabde. Social media starts the conversation, she says, yet few respondents named it: the survey records how audiences believe they decide, while the industry sees what starts the conversation.

Redefining Success

So does a film’s journey end when it leaves the theatre? First, success is now measured more widely. Second, OTT can give a film a second life: Dhurandhar was revived, Raja Shivaji found a second audience, Dune returned in overseas rankings. Third, audiences treat cinemas as an option, kept for films with scale, company and experience. The theatrical run is now the first chapter of a longer story.

The box office only opens the innings. OTT decides how long a film plays on.
